Website Content Study

What the research says, what the site says, and what it should say

Internal · August 2026

The problem in one sentence

The site's most-read lines — hero, meta description, footer — are poetic where they should be concrete, and advertise services Laawol does not offer. A first-time visitor cannot tell, in ten seconds, what they can actually do here.

1 · What the research says

You have ten seconds

Nielsen Norman Group's analysis of over 2 billion page visits found visitors typically leave within 10–20 seconds — unless the page communicates a clear value proposition in the first ten, in which case they often stay minutes. The first screen either answers "what can I do here?" or the visitor is gone.

Concrete beats vague — measurably

Packard & Berger's study of concreteness in customer-facing language found that more concrete wording raised purchase intent by up to 12% and actual purchases by 13–30%, across channels and languages. The century-old craft rule agrees — Hopkins (1923): platitudes roll off; specific facts land with full weight.

The working formula

Headline = the concrete end-benefit in one sentence. Subheadline = what you offer, for whom, and what makes it trustworthy. Nothing on the first screen that requires interpretation.

3 · What the site says today — and what's wrong with it

REMOVEServices Laawol does not offer

The boilerplate promises food, restaurants, home cooks, lawyers, and professional services. The platform offers none of these — the real catalog is barrel shipping, freight, car sales, car transport, and car parking. This isn't just unclear; a visitor who signs up to find a restaurant discovers the site "lied" to them, and Stanford's web-credibility work is blunt about how fast an overclaim destroys trust. It appears in at least:

  • The meta description of the homepage (what Google shows)
  • The hero subheadline ("…expédition, voitures, fret, restauration et aide professionnelle")
  • The footer boilerplate on every page ("…shipping, cars, freight, food, and professional services")
  • about.html, app.html, contact.html (…"Cars, barrels, food, and professionals organized together", "Find food", "Find a pro", "African food & restaurants", "home cooks")

REWRITEThe hero forces interpretation

"Find the right business for the road home."

It's evocative, but it fails the ten-second test: it names no service, no action, no place. "The road home" only means something once you already know that Laawol is Pular for the road — which a first-time visitor doesn't.

KEEP — but relocatedThe tagline itself

"Laawol — the road home." is genuinely good brand copy — it's the meaning of the name. It belongs in the footer as a signature, with its story attached, not in the H1 doing the explanatory work it can't do.

DECIDEShared barrels on services.html

The services page advertises "Barils partagés" while the feature is switched off platform-wide (sharedBarrelsEnabled). Either hide the section until launch or mark it "coming soon" — right now it promises a service no customer can request.

Why this works: the headline now passes the ten-second test (verb, services, corridor — Taptap Send's exact pattern), every claim matches a service that actually exists in the app, the concrete price-per-barrel/per-kilo language is the kind shown to lift purchases 13–30%, and the brand poetry keeps its place — explained, in the footer, where it builds identity instead of costing clarity.

5 · If you do only one thing

Remove the fictional services. Unclear copy costs conversions; untrue copy costs trust — and Laawol's entire pitch is trust.
